# Lesson 1: NIS2 Article 21 — The Ten Mandatory Cybersecurity Measures

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the ten cybersecurity measures mandated by NIS2 Article 21(2) and their legal basis
- Understand the scope of "essential" and "important" entities and why proportionality applies
- Explain the governance responsibility placed on management bodies under Article 21(4)
- Recognise how Article 21 relates to the broader NIS2 risk management framework

## What Is NIS2 and Why Does Article 21 Matter?

The Network and Information Security Directive 2 (NIS2, Directive 2022/2555/EU) replaces the original NIS Directive and significantly expands both the scope of entities covered and the depth of cybersecurity obligations imposed on them. Where NIS1 applied to a narrow set of operators of essential services, NIS2 reaches across eighteen sectors — energy, transport, banking, health, digital infrastructure, public administration, and more — and introduces two entity categories: **essential entities** (subject to proactive supervision) and **important entities** (subject to reactive supervision).

Article 21 is the operational core of NIS2. It mandates that all in-scope entities adopt "appropriate and proportionate technical, operational, and organisational measures" to manage the risks posed to their network and information systems. Crucially, Article 21(4) places direct accountability on the management body: senior leadership must approve cybersecurity risk management measures, oversee their implementation, and can be held personally liable for infringements. This is a deliberate policy choice — cybersecurity is no longer a technical department concern; it is a board-level obligation.

## The Ten Measures Explained

Article 21(2) enumerates ten categories of cybersecurity measures. Every in-scope entity must address all ten, with the depth of implementation proportionate to the entity's size, risk exposure, and sector.

**(a) Policies on risk analysis and information system security** — The organisation must maintain documented, regularly reviewed policies that define how cyber risks are identified, assessed, and treated. This is the foundational governance layer: without it, the remaining nine measures have no structured basis.

**(b) Incident handling** — The entity must implement capability to detect, classify, respond to, and report security incidents. This includes documented procedures, defined roles and escalation paths, tested playbooks, and the notification processes required under Article 23 (early warning within 24 hours; detailed report within 72 hours for significant incidents).

**(c) Business continuity** — Backup management, disaster recovery, and crisis management procedures must be in place. This includes defining Recovery Time Objectives (RTO) and Recovery Point Objectives (RPO) for critical systems, and testing recovery procedures regularly.

**(d) Supply chain security** — The entity must address cybersecurity risks arising from relationships with direct suppliers and service providers. This requires supplier risk assessments, contractual security clauses, and ongoing monitoring of the supply chain's security posture.

**(e) Security in network and information systems acquisition, development, and maintenance** — Secure development and procurement practices must be embedded. This covers vulnerability management, patch management, secure coding standards, and security requirements in procurement specifications.

**(f) Policies and procedures to assess the effectiveness of cybersecurity risk management measures** — The entity must have mechanisms to evaluate whether its controls are working. This includes internal audits, penetration testing, and metrics that feed back into the risk management cycle.

**(g) Basic cyber hygiene practices and cybersecurity training** — All staff must receive role-appropriate cybersecurity training. Basic hygiene practices — strong authentication, software updates, phishing awareness, endpoint protection — must be institutionalised.

**(h) Policies and procedures regarding the use of cryptography and, where appropriate, encryption** — The entity must define when and how cryptographic controls are applied to protect data at rest and in transit, covering key management, algorithm standards, and certificate lifecycle management.

**(i) Human resources security, access control policies, and asset management** — Personnel security (background checks, role-based access, offboarding procedures), access control (least privilege, MFA, privileged access management), and asset inventory management must all be addressed.

**(j) The use of multi-factor authentication or continuous authentication solutions, secure voice, video, and text communications, and secured emergency communication systems where appropriate** — MFA is explicitly mandated for all access to network and information systems where it is technically feasible, with special attention to administrative and remote access.

## Key Takeaways

- Article 21(2) defines ten non-negotiable cybersecurity measure categories — all ten must be addressed by every in-scope entity.
- Management bodies bear direct accountability; "I delegated it to IT" is not a valid defence under NIS2.
- Proportionality allows smaller or lower-risk entities to implement lighter controls, but proportionality must be documented and justified — it does not mean optional.
- The measures are interdependent: a gap in (a) — risk analysis — undermines the coherence of all other controls.
- NIS2 enforcement in Albania sits with AKCESK; inspections can request documented evidence of all ten measure categories.
